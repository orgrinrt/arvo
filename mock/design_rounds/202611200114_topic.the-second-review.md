## Where the arm lives, and what the design says about it

The design says the format cannot take the room arm itself, because its step is total over every slot and
a bound over the range would need a test of the operands. The review points out that the format already
has `Member<F>`, a slot that is a member by its type, and a step over two of those would carry the same
bound with no test. So the sentence calls a gap in the format's surface an impossibility. What is true is
narrower: the format's steps take any slot, so the arm is not theirs to take as they stand, and a value holds
its member in a carrier rather than as a `Member`, so reaching a step over members would mean landing each
operand as one, which today runs the format's applied map over a four-limb position.

Both reviews read the canon as silent on which crate owns the arm and both point at the format, by the rule
that a crate holds only its own thing and by the format's own design, which says the map performs the exact
step in a domain chosen to hold it. Moving the arm is a design for `arvo-format` with a landing of its own to
price, and the canon's silence makes it a question rather than this round's to settle, so it is filed as one,
`where_the_exact_step_formed_in_the_host_integer_lives_the_fo`, with the two options. This round only makes
the sentence true.

The same paragraph says a product is "one 128-bit division" where the bound holds. Where the terms fit 64
bits the division is the machine's 64-bit one, which the same section says a few lines further on; the
sentence says the host's integer instead.

## Which shapes take which arm

The proof deep dive says every width up to 8 and most of the wider ones take the first arm. At every width
the highest and the deepest exponent of each family take the four limbs, since the radix to the exponent is
past 128 bits there, and the product at exponent zero of the unsigned 64-bit range does too, since the
square of its widest member reaches `2^127`. The sentence is rewritten to say which exponents take which arm.

## The predicates at their edge, at every radix

The suites cannot tell the arms apart, because both equal the oracle, so a predicate stricter than the room
leaves every suite green, and only the `bound` module ties the predicates to the room. It held them only at
radix two. The module now holds `PRODUCT` and `QUOTIENT` at the room's last exponent and one past it, and the
conversion's `HOLDS` from exponent zero onto the room's last exponent and one past, at 8, 16, 32 and 64 bits,
both ranges, radix two, ten and three. The edges come from the tests' own arithmetic in `common::edges`,
which the module takes by path, with the widest member moved there from `common::shapes` so the file stands
alone.
