# Topic: the N-dimension and shape design

**Date:** 2026-07-28
**Phase:** TOPIC
**Scope:** the design talk for `arvo-ndim` and `arvo-shape`. This file is the transcript: decisions
are recorded here as they are made.
**Rests on:** `202607281126_topic.what-arvo-ships-toward-n-dimensions.md` (the research) and
`202607281127_topic.arvo-ndim-and-shape.md` (op's steer and the open questions).

## The premise, cleared before the talk starts

The research flagged one question as blocking and unanswerable from documents: loimu designed a
fixed-point geometry crate over these exact arvo primitives and then abandoned it for `kurbo` and
`peniko`, which is the design this workspace has just committed to.

**op has answered, and the reversal was not technical.** loimu was over-extended, trying to be
hilavitkutin, hökkeli, tassu and an application framework simultaneously. Under that load it drifted
toward floats and existing frameworks in order to assemble a framework from pieces that already
existed, which op identified as backwards and corrected by refocusing into this workspace. Relaxing
to std, alloc and floats left it with no reason to exist, since such frameworks already exist;
unrelaxed, it could not be built until the stack underneath it did. The bench evidence from that era
was one-off sketches predating mockspace's harness and is **lost**.

So the reversal is collateral of an over-extension the current split exists to correct. It is not
evidence against fixed-point geometry, and `arvo-shape` proceeds. Full record in hökkeli's
`202607281214_topic.hokkeli-is-host-driven-and-owns-only-the-shell.md`, D13.

## One correction to the research, found while preparing the talk

`arvo-tensor`'s design says it "sits at L2 as a peer of `arvo-bitmask`", with the division by element
domain: bits on one side, values on the other, and "no dependency on `arvo-bitmask` or any other L1 /
L2 crate".

**They are not peers.** `arvo-bitmask/Cargo.toml` lists `arvo-tensor` among its dependencies, because
`Capacity`, `ConstCapacity`, `Dim`, `cap` and `cap_size` all live in `arvo-tensor` and `BitMatrix<W,
C: Capacity>` binds them. The bit containers sit above the value containers, and the only reason is
where the capacity foundation happens to live.

That edge is accidental rather than meant, and it is the sharpest argument in the whole question for
lifting the foundation out from under both.

## The taxonomy walk

op asked for the whole arvo taxonomy before placing the shape crates. The walk found that the shape
question is the second structural problem here, not the first.

### The facade is load-bearing, and arvo's own rules already forbid it

Every `forbidden-imports` rule in `mockspace.toml`, from `arvo-transparent` through `arvo-hash`,
lists `arvo::*` as forbidden. **The declared design is that the facade is terminal and nothing
depends on it.** Eight crates carry a Cargo dependency on `arvo` anyway: `arvo-bits`, `arvo-tensor`,
`arvo-bitmask`, `arvo-hash`, `arvo-sparse`, `arvo-comb`, `arvo-graph`, `arvo-spectral`.

The lints only ever read each crate's `src/lib.rs`, so `use arvo::USize;` in a module file was never
seen. That is the bulk of the 52 `forbidden-imports` findings recorded in
`202607270711_topic.the-lints-can-see-the-whole-crate-now.md`.

### It is not only drift. One rule is unsatisfiable as written

`arvo-tensor`'s rule forbids `arvo::*` and states it "depends on arvo-storage, arvo-bits-contracts and
arvo-numeric-contracts only". But `Capacity::CAP` is a `Cap`, and `Cap` lives in the facade. **The
layer rule and the type placement cannot both hold.** The algorithm crates inherit the same problem
through `UFixed` and `IFixed`, which also live in the facade.

op's reading, recorded: arvo has been working fine, so the wrong shape is mostly benign in practice.
It remains a soundness issue and a design contradiction that should not exist, and the lints were
there precisely to prevent it, so the standing vibe check of "if mockspace allows it, it is fine" was
resting on a gate that could not see. **The fix self-heals once the crate purposes actually hold and
the facade is the leaf downstream of everything.**

### A correction to an earlier claim in this round

`arvo-bitmask` depending on `arvo-tensor` was called an accidental edge. It is not: bitmask's rule
explicitly permits `arvo-tensor` and its stated reason names it. What remains true is why the edge
exists, which is that `BitMatrix<W, C: Capacity>` needs the capacity system and the capacity system
lives in the value-container crate.

### The anomaly that explains the whole thing

arvo's convention is a contracts crate plus a concrete sibling: `arvo-bits-contracts` to `arvo-bits`,
`arvo-mask-contracts` to `arvo-bitmask`. `arvo-numeric-contracts` has no sibling, and its own README
says so: "Default impl bodies live in the `arvo` facade."

**The facade became load-bearing because it is standing in for a crate that was never created.**
Creating that crate completes a pattern that already holds everywhere else rather than inventing one.

## Decisions

Recorded as they are made. Nothing here is the agent's call.

### D1. The dimensional foundation is lifted out from under the container crates

**Decision (op).** It becomes its own crate below `arvo-tensor` and `arvo-bitmask` rather than staying
inside `arvo-tensor` or moving into `arvo-storage`, so the container crates become peers over one
foundation instead of one of them hosting it for the others.

### D2. Four new crates, and the facade becomes a leaf

**Decision (op).** The bunching, with capacity and shape split rather than merged.

| Crate | Holds | Status |
|---|---|---|
| `arvo-capacity` | `Cap`, `Capacity`, `ConstCapacity`, `Dim<N>`, `cap`, `cap_size` | new |
| `arvo-shape` | rank, per-axis extent sequences, generic over rank | new |
| `arvo-numeric` | `UFixed`, `IFixed`, `Int`, `Uint`, `FastFloat`, `StrictFloat`, and the default contract impls | new |
| `arvo-geom` | `Point`, extent, box, `Affine`, and the semantic aliases | new |
| `arvo` | re-exports, and nothing else | becomes terminal |

**`Cap` goes to `arvo-capacity`, not to `arvo-storage`.** `Cap` is the value form of a capacity,
`Capacity` the type form, `Dim<N>` the marker, and `cap` and `cap_size` the bridges between value and
type. That is one tight semantic bunch, and placing `Cap` with the const-generic carriers in
`arvo-storage` would separate it from the traits that give it meaning.

**Capacity and shape stay separate.** A single axis extent is a workaround for a compiler limitation,
and a sequence of them is a real concept. Merging them leaves the workaround without a boundary
around it, and `Array<T, C>` already demonstrates a consumer that wants capacity with no notion of
rank.

**`arvo-capacity` does not depend on `arvo-numeric`.** It needs `USize` for `Cap` and nothing else, so
it sits as a sibling of `arvo-bits-contracts` rather than below it, and the two new middle crates are
independent of one another.

**`Enumerator` leaves `arvo-tensor`.** A blanket trait over any `IntoIterator` is not a container
concern. Where it lands is not settled here.

### D3. Naming, settled by the bunching

**Decision (op), implied by D2 and recorded explicitly.** `arvo-shape` is the **rank and extent
system**, which is what shape means in both mathematics and computing. The spatial crate is
**`arvo-geom`**, which is the standard name for that job and the one loimu used.

This reverses the working assumption carried from `202607281127`, where `arvo-shape` was to be the
spatial crate and the foundation was to be called `arvo-ndim`. `arvo-ndim` is dropped: it abbreviates
where no other arvo crate does, and the crate it named holds capacity as much as dimensionality.

### D4. A shape is a cons-list of capacities

**Decision (op).** Rank and per-axis extents are carried by a type-level cons-list whose elements are
capacities. Rank is the list length, extents are the elements, and the backing storage is the
recursive composition of each capacity's array.

```
Nil::Array<E>        = E                       rank 0 is a scalar
Cons<H, T>::Array<E> = H::Array<T::Array<E>>   one more axis
```

**Why it survives the constraint that rules out the alternatives.** Rank and element count are
**associated consts**, so the arithmetic happens in value position and no const expression reaches
type position. That is the exact failure `Capacity` exists to prevent, and it is what makes a scheme
that multiplies extents into a stride or a flat length unusable.

**What it gives beyond rank**, and this is why op chose it rather than tuples: the cons-list also
carries membership and satisfaction checks, and later supports inferring things a naive typestate
cannot represent. Rank is the smallest thing it does.

**The existing types become instantiations rather than migrations.** `Matrix<W, C>` is already
`C::Array<C::Array<W>>`, which is `Cons<C, Cons<C, Nil>>::Array<W>`. Per-axis extents come free, so a
rectangle becomes expressible where `Matrix`'s single squared capacity cannot express one.

**Rejected:** a tuple of capacities, which needs impls generated per arity and caps rank at whatever
is written. A capacity plus a rank marker, which forces every axis to share an extent and so cannot
express a rectangle, which is the limitation that started this. Keeping bare recursive nesting, which
leaves a function generic over rank unwritable, the one thing actually missing.

### D5. The cons-list is extracted to notko, as its own crate

**Decision (op).** The pattern is reinvented per repository today and is about to be reinvented a
third time, so it is formalised once in notko as its own crate in the notko repository.

**The duplication, with citations.** `hilavitkutin/mock/crates/hilavitkutin-api/src/access.rs:50` and
`kolli/mock/crates/kolli-api/src/lib.rs:143` both declare `pub struct Empty;` and
`pub struct Cons<H, T>(core::marker::PhantomData<(H, T)>);`, byte-identical. Each has then regrown
the same operations under different names: hilavitkutin has `AccessSet` (the length fold),
`Contains`, `ContainsAll` and `Concat`; kolli has `CommandSet` with `LEN`, `each` and `find`.

**Length is parameterised rather than dropped or hardcoded.** notko cannot name arvo's `USize`, since
arvo depends on notko. op's resolution is to make the count generic, and a finding while checking it
is what makes that the only workable shape:

> **A separate counting crate in arvo is blocked by the orphan rule.** If notko declares both the
> trait and the list types, an impl in arvo has two foreign items and cannot be written. Only notko
> can implement a notko trait for notko types.

So the count parameterises in notko and each consumer implements the property for its own type,
which is legal because the type is theirs:

```rust
// notko declares the trait and the blanket fold
pub trait Cardinal { const ZERO: Self; fn succ(self) -> Self; }
pub trait Length<N: Cardinal> { const LEN: N; }
impl<N: Cardinal, H, T: Length<N>> Length<N> for Cons<H, T> { const LEN: N = T::LEN.succ(); }

// arvo implements a notko trait for a local type. Legal.
impl Cardinal for USize { .. }
```

One crate rather than two, no orphan problem, and the count type stays the consumer's choice. The
cost is that the successor must be const-callable, so `Cardinal` is a const trait and notko gates
`const_trait_impl` as arvo already does.

**Recorded because it has no home yet:** notko has no `mock/` directory and does not run mockspace,
so this decision has no design round on the notko side to live in. It is recorded here, where the
talk happened.

### D6. The trait a count type implements is `Cardinal`

**Decision (op).** Not `Countable`: the `-able` suffix means "can be counted", and this type **is**
the count, so the suffix inverts the meaning. Not `Sized`, which is in `core` rather than `std` and
means "this type has a known size", where what is wanted is "this type can stand for the size of
something else".

**Not `Natural` either, and the reason is a placement argument rather than a naming one.** Zero and
successor are the Peano constructors, so `Natural` is exactly precise, and that is the problem: it
claims a mathematical primitive, which is arvo's territory rather than notko's. notko's existing
traits are properties, `Boundable` and `NonZeroable`, and `Cardinal` joins that family by naming a
property a type can have rather than declaring what the type is.

It also leaves `Natural` available to arvo, which is where op noted it would sound at home, alongside
the semantic aliases and consts arvo already carries.

### D7. Each domain aliases the cell and the leaf to its own semantics

**Decision (op).** The shared crate ships `Cons` and the leaf. Every consumer aliases both to the
words that fit its domain, so the pattern reads as the thing it is modelling rather than as a
list. The generic names appear at the definition and essentially nowhere else.

| Domain | Cell | Leaf | Reads as |
|---|---|---|---|
| `arvo-shape` | `Axis<H, T>` | `Scalar` | an axis of 3, then an axis of 4, then a scalar |
| `hilavitkutin` | `Access<H, T>` | `Deny` (op: "or whatever is the opposite best") | an access to A, then to B, then deny |
| `kolli` | `Command<H, T>` | `EmptyInvoke` (op: "or whatever") | a command, then a command, then nothing to invoke |

```rust
type Vec3  = Axis<Dim<3>, Scalar>;
type Rect2 = Axis<Dim<3>, Axis<Dim<4>, Scalar>>;
```

Rank 0 being `Scalar` is not a pun. A shape with no axes is a scalar, and the alias states it.

The hilavitkutin and kolli leaf names are op's sketches rather than settled, and each repository
confirms its own pair when it adopts the extracted crate. What is settled is that they alias rather
than use the generic names directly.

### D8. Bits are not forced onto the shape abstraction

**Decision (op).** Where the shape fits the bit containers, they alias it accordingly. Where it does
not fit, they do the thing that fits instead. **Reuse is not forced.**

The question this answers: the same shape has different storage projections per element domain. A
dense value shape composes to a nested array, `[[T; 4]; 3]`. A bit shape does not want
`[[bool; 4]; 3]`; it wants the innermost axis packed into words, `[Mask<4>; 3]`. And `Csr` cannot be
a nested array at all, because the whole point of sparse storage is that it does not mirror the
shape.

So the shape vocabulary is available to bits and to sparse, and neither is obliged to take it. A
projection that has to be contorted to fit is evidence the fit is wrong, not a problem to engineer
around.

### D9. The crate is `notko-hlist`, and prose still says cons-list

**Decision (op).** The extracted crate in the notko repository is named `notko-hlist`.

**No amendment to `vocabulary.md` is needed**, because the rule is not in conflict. It is a
documentation rule, unifying one term so that prose does not mix several names for one thing. A crate
name is an identifier rather than prose. The crate is `notko-hlist`; prose continues to say cons-list.

**On which term is more prevalent, since it came up.** `HList` is the established name for this
specific thing, heterogeneous and type-level: Kiselyov, Lämmel and Schupke's 2004 "Strongly typed
heterogeneous collections", carried since by `frunk` in Rust, `shapeless` in Scala and the Haskell
`HList` library. `cons-list` is older and broader, from Lisp, and denotes any singly-linked list of
cons cells without implying heterogeneity or type-level residence.

Worth knowing for the prose side: in Rust specifically, "cons list" is the Rust book's term for a
**runtime** linked list, the `enum List { Cons(i32, Box<List>), Nil }` example in the smart-pointer
chapter. So a Rust reader's first association with the phrase is a heap-allocated structure, which is
close to the opposite of what this crate ships. The rule stands as op's call; the ambiguity is
recorded so a future reader of the prose knows it is there.

**Rejected on the way:** `notko-cons`, which names the cell or the verb rather than the structure;
`notko-cons-list`, too verbose; `notko-typelist`, which op read as hype; and `notko-chain`,
`notko-spine` and `notko-row`, none of which say heterogeneous and type-level as directly as the term
of art does.

### D10. Rotation is grounded on rotors, extending to motors

**Decision (op).** Rotation is expressed as **rotors** from geometric algebra, not as quaternions and
not as matrices, with **motors** from projective geometric algebra as the extension covering rigid
motion. ikiuni needs the same machinery, so this is not a geom-only call.

**Why quaternions are not the grounding.** They do not generalise: quaternions are a four-dimensional
algebra special to 3D, where unit quaternions double-cover SO(3). What generalises is the matrix they
convert to, and a matrix is a poor general form, carrying N squared components for n(n-1)/2 degrees of
freedom and drifting under repeated composition.

**Rotors are the general form, and the familiar cases fall out of it.** A rotor lives in the even
subalgebra of the Clifford algebra: at rank 2 that subalgebra is the complex numbers and a rotation
is one angle; at rank 3 it is the quaternions; at rank N a rotor carries a scalar plus n(n-1)/2
bivector components, which is exactly the degrees of freedom a rotation has.

**Motors extend it to rigid motion**, rotation and translation in one composable object. The same
pattern repeats: a dual quaternion is the 3D motor, already the industry answer for skinning, and a
motor is its general form. `Affine` is a rigid motion plus scale, so this is directly the type geom
wants. Lineage: Gunn's plane-based projective geometric algebra, with Dorst and Lasenby.

**The component count needs care but is not a blocker.** A rotor's 1 + n(n-1)/2 components is a
computed extent, and putting that in type position is the const-expression hazard `Capacity` exists
to avoid. op's correction, recorded: the toolchain is nightly with a vetted set of unstable features,
so this is not the wall the agent described. The fallback is in any case the hlist itself, since a
bivector basis is the set of 2-subsets of the axes and so is derivable by a type-level fold rather
than by arithmetic in type position.

### D11. Curves are arvo's, and the direction is a bench matrix rather than a pick

**Decision (op).** Curves are unambiguously arvo territory, being mathematics. The representation is
**not chosen up front**: the candidates are benched, and the winner per workload is selected
statically so LLVM erases the alternatives rather than the code branching at runtime. That is
`arvo-strategy`'s existing job applied one level up.

The candidates to bench: Euler spirals and clothoids, biarcs and piecewise-circular, implicit
distance fields, and classical Beziers.

**The workloads are already named, and they differ.** Rounded corners want exact arcs, where a Bezier
only approximates. ikiuni needs fixed-point rotations first class for heavy simulation. A terrain
editor wants splines, and op's read that Bezier is not best even there is well founded, since a
Bezier's control points do not lie on the curve, which is precisely what an author wants.

**A research lead, unidentified.** op has a hunch about unconventional wins from the **nonlinear
algebra** side, from a name not yet recalled. Candidates offered and rejected as not it:
**Hackbusch** (hierarchical matrices and tensor formats), **Heckbert** (adaptive subdivision and
flatness testing), **Hausdorff** (the metric by which curve-approximation error is bounded). Further
candidates for the research pass, none confirmed: **Eckart** (Eckart-Young-Mirsky, provably optimal
low-rank approximation with a bound, which matches the "bounded optimal" framing), **Sturmfels** (the
central figure in nonlinear algebra), **Hankel**, **Higham**, **Householder**. Recorded because the
next step for arvo is research before committing to any direction.

### D12. Colour is not geometry. Packing stays in arvo; an imaging domain takes the rest

**Decision (op).** The split holds: bit-level packing, encoding and representation are already arvo's,
through `arvo-bits`, `arvo-bitmask` and `arvo-hash`, since a pixel format is a bit layout. Colour as a
perceptual domain is not numerics with a colour flavour, and does not belong in `arvo-geom`.

The bundle around colour is **imaging**: a grid of samples, what a sample means, how samples are laid
out, and how geometry becomes samples. It expands incrementally toward completeness rather than
needing to be whole at once. Linebender converged on the same grouping and the same name.

Compression is a third question, closer to `arvo-comb`'s neighbourhood than to colour's, and is not
settled here. `lato` was considered and rejected as a home: layout and relativity have no relationship
to colour at all.

### D13. Algebraic structure: the gap, and where the pieces already live

**Raised by op.** arvo lacks first-class monoids, monads, folding in general, and folding across ranks
(cascades). The placement question is whether that is one new crate or falls across crates by domain.

The pieces mostly have homes already, and the analysis is recorded so the remaining gap is visible:

- **Type-level folds are already in the hlist.** `Length`, `Concat` and `ContainsAll` are folds. A
  general `Fold` belongs in `notko-hlist` and needs no numerics.
- **The monad half already exists in notko**, as `Maybe`, `Outcome` and the `ConstTry` machinery.
  arvo does not need to restate it.
- **Functor over containers** is shape-preserving `map`, so it lives with the containers.
- **Cascades need the shape**, since reducing along an axis requires knowing which axis, so they
  belong to `arvo-shape` and `arvo-tensor`.
- **Flattening a nested container is `Concat` on its shape.** The monadic operation at the value level
  is the type-level concat at the shape level: one operation seen from two heights.

**The genuine gap is the algebraic contracts themselves**, `Semigroup`, `Monoid` and whatever of the
ladder is wanted. `arvo-numeric-contracts` is operations (`Abs`, `Recip`, `Sqrt`, `TotalOrd`), which
is a different thing from algebraic structure, so they have no home today.

**One mechanism worth noting, because arvo already has the idiom.** A type is a monoid more than once,
under addition and under multiplication, and a type may carry only one impl of a trait. Haskell wraps
in `Sum` and `Product` newtypes. arvo already parameterises by ZST marker, as `Hot`, `Warm`, `Cold`
and `Precise`, so `Monoid<Add>` and `Monoid<Mul>` follow the pattern already present and need no
wrapper types.

Where the contracts crate sits, and whether it is one crate or an extension of an existing one, is
not settled.
