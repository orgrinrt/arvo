### The forks, in the order they have to be answered

**S1. Do the landing policies live in the type, or on the operation?** The two industrial systems that
have thought hardest about this disagree outright. SystemC (IEEE 1666) puts them in the type:
`sc_fixed<W, I, Q, O, N>` carries quantization and overflow as type parameters, so the policy travels
with the value. MATLAB splits them: `numerictype` carries width, scaling and signedness, while a
separate `fimath` object carries `RoundingMethod`, `OverflowAction`, `ProductMode` and `SumMode`, so
the policy travels with the arithmetic rather than the value. This is the fork with the widest blast
radius in the whole round, because it decides whether two values that round differently can be added
at all, how wide every signature is, and whether a preset is a property of a number or of a context.

**S2. Is there a composition type, or is the composition a set of bounds?** A struct
(`Whatever<A, B, C, ...>`) that the semantic names alias, against keeping `UFixed` / `IFixed` and the
float wrappers as the concrete types with the axes reaching them as trait bounds and associated types.
D52 says compositions are public and bindable, which needs them nameable, but nameable is satisfied by
either shape.

**S3. If a composition type exists: its name, its parameter list, its order, its defaults.** `Num` is
mine and carries no thought. The order matters because it is what a consumer reads.

**S4. Is signedness part of the format or a sibling axis?** Mathematically the format is the set of
representable values, and whether negatives are in that set is a statement about the set, which argues
signedness belongs inside the format. Structurally it sits on `Bits` today as its own marker, and the
law sketch conditions on the `(Overflow, Signedness)` pair, which reads more naturally if signedness is
a sibling. Both readings are defensible and they produce different types.

**S5. Does the container stay a projection, or become a parameter?** Today `BitsContainerFor` projects
(strategy, width, sign) onto a carrier, so the container is derived and invisible. Growth changes what
the projection has to serve, since an intermediate needs a wider carrier than the value.

**S6. Where does underflow sit?** Nested inside the floating format as the sketch has it, or a
top-level axis that fixed formats ignore. Nesting keeps fixed types from carrying a meaningless
parameter; a top-level axis keeps the axis list flat and uniform.

The per-axis instance questions follow these, in the order already tabled: the scale (A1), the format
decomposition (A2), rounding (A3), overflow (A4), growth (A5), then the set as a whole (A6).

### A1. The scale question, whether a format can have a denominator of `2^F - 1`

This row is not carried forward from any earlier file. It surfaced from reading
`sketches/202607300500_format-as-exponent-function/01_format_trait.rs` against D36, and it says a
question two files record as settled is not settled.

**The problem.** The sketched `Format` is Flocq's exponent function and nothing else: `Fixed<F>`
returns `-F`, `Floating<PREC, EMIN, U>` defers to an underflow policy, and a value is representable
when it equals an integer significand times the radix raised to that exponent. Every representable
value is therefore `m * 2^e`.

UNORM is not of that form. Its value is `k / (2^F - 1)`, and at `F = 8` the denominator is 255, which
is not a power of two and never will be. No exponent function produces it. So the format as sketched
cannot express UNORM at all.

That matters because two prior files record the question as answered. D36 (`202607291800`) decided
"arvo takes UNORM, or something to the same effect" on the reasoning that the encoding is a property
the type does not currently carry, and `202607300400` lists D36 under "answered rather than reopened
by this proposal", stating the encoding is a format parameter. It is a format parameter only if the
format has a parameter that can hold it, and the one sketched does not. The answer was recorded
against a mechanism that had not been checked.

**What the field calls the general form.** MATLAB's Fixed-Point Designer and Simulink both define
fixed point by a two-part scaling rather than a radix point. Quoted from the Simulink scaling page:
`real-world value = (slope * integer) + bias`, where `slope = slope adjustment factor * 2^fixed
exponent`, the adjustment factor is "a number greater than or equal to 1 and less than 2", the slope
"must be a positive number", and the bias "can be any number". The same page states the special case
directly: "A binary-point scaled number is equal to a slope-bias scaled number where the bias is 0 and
the slope adjustment factor is 1." The stated reason for the general form is "when you need non power
of two scaling, if your hardware uses a custom format, or when your real-world data doesn't start at
zero".

UNORM is exactly a slope-bias number. Its slope is `1 / (2^F - 1)`, which at `F = 8` decomposes as
`(256 / 255) * 2^-8`, with an adjustment factor of `256/255` sitting inside the required `[1, 2)`, and
a bias of zero. It is not an exotic encoding; it is one value of a parameter the field has had for
decades and arvo has never named.

So this is the same shape of gap `202607300200` already found for rounding. The axis exists in
industry, it is absent from arvo, and its absence was invisible because nothing in arvo could state
it. Flocq does not cover it either: `generic_format` fixes the scale at a power of the radix, so
adopting a general scale is going past the formalization rather than instantiating it, which is what
op asked for.

**What adopting a scale buys, beyond UNORM.** Three things, all of which the round already wants.

Number-system membership stops being a hand-written table and becomes derivable. `202607291900`'s
finding lists per-type inhabitance by hand: `UFixed<I, 0>` is natural, `UFixed<I, F>` with `F > 0` is
dyadic, and so on. With a scale in the type, the rule is one line: the values inhabit the dyadic
rationals when the adjustment factor is one, and the rationals otherwise. UNORM's row falls out rather
than being appended.

The MV-chain gets a home. `202607300300` identified `UFixed<0, F>` under the UNORM encoding as a
finite Łukasiewicz chain, the standard MV-algebra, and under arvo's own encoding as a half-open
interval with no name in the literature. With a scale parameter, the difference between the two is one
parameter value, and the structural consequence (one is an MV-chain, the other is not) is derived by
D51's mechanism rather than declared for a bespoke type.

D36 gets a mechanism rather than a restatement. `UFixed<0, F>` having no multiplicative identity
becomes a fact about a scale rather than a fact about the type, and `OneRepresentable` starts asking
the right question.

**What a bias costs, stated because it is the deciding factor.** Slope and bias are not equally
cheap. A scale is closed under addition: `S*qa + S*qb = S*(qa + qb)`, so the sum is a value of the
same format and every law the round derives survives untouched. A bias is not: `(S*qa + B) + (S*qb +
B) = S*(qa + qb) + 2B`, which carries twice the bias and is therefore a value of a different format.
Addition on a biased format does not close, so `Semigroup<Add>` would not resolve for it.

Read one way that is a defect. Read the other way it is the design working: the absence of an impl
carries a mathematical fact, exactly as `OneRepresentable`'s absence does, and a consumer who folds a
biased format learns at compile time that the operation they wrote is not the operation they meant.
The cost is real either way, because a type that cannot be folded is a type most of layer 1 does not
reach.

**Reframed, after op's steer on the first attempt.** The fork was first put with the scale carried as
integer associated consts (`SCALE_NUM`, `SCALE_DEN`). That framing was wrong on its own terms, and
wrong in a way this round has already corrected once elsewhere. D2 of `202607282100` moved the
container tag off exactly this shape: "the tag, currently an integer computed by a function, is a
closed vocabulary of slots and becomes an enum of types". Putting the scale back into integers repeats
the mistake the same round removed.

The shipped idiom is uniform and the fork has to be stated inside it. A policy axis is a ZST marker;
the marker implements a `pub const trait` that projects its answer as an associated type; the answer
is a type rather than a number; and a fact about the combination is carried by the presence or absence
of an impl rather than by a value anyone reads. `Capacity` does this (`type Array<T>`, with `Dim<N>`
as the marker and the capacity itself a type). `BitsContainerFor` does this (`type T`, the container).
`Underflow` in the format sketch already does this (`Unbounded` / `Gradual` / `Flushed` as markers).
`OneRepresentable` does the absence half. The law sketch does both at once, conditioning a blanket
impl on a type-level pair.

Const generics stay where they are already the right form. `Fixed<F>` keeps a const `F` because a
fractional-bit count is a number the consumer writes; what becomes typestate is the *choice of
scaling*, which is a policy and not a number. The two are different questions and the first framing
collapsed them.

Where a type-level number is genuinely needed, `Dim<N>` is the shipped type-form of one, and going
from a const parameter to it is the per-width impl table D2 already settled and
`arvo-compile-time-last.md` already licenses.

**Second steer, and the finding it produced.** Op's objection to the reframed fork: the typestate
proof was sitting in comments rather than in machinery. That is correct and it is the sharper of the
two objections, because it is not a presentation problem. Writing `impl Dyadic for Radix {}` by hand
is an **asserted** property in D16's sense (`202607290200`), and D16 says an asserted property is an
`unsafe impl` carrying a contract, because nothing stops it being wrong. Every option as first stated
had membership asserted per marker, which means the crate would ship a pile of unchecked promises
about mathematics and call it typestate.

What is wanted is a **derived** property in D16's sense: computed from the thing itself, unable to
lie, a plain safe impl. So the question stops being "which marker vocabulary" and becomes "what
representation makes the properties structural".

The answer is to carry the scale as **the constructor that built it** rather than as a number or as an
opaque pair. A denominator written `Pow2<F>` cannot be anything other than a power of two, because
that is what the constructor means; there is no inhabitant of the type that violates it. So
`impl PowerOfTwo for Pow2<F>` is not a claim about an opaque value, it is a restatement of the
constructor's definition, and everything conditioned on it is derived. A denominator written
`Mersenne<F>` is `2^F - 1` by construction, odd and greater than one for every `F > 1`, so it is
provably not a power of two and correctly gets no such impl. A consumer's arbitrary `Exact<N>` proves
nothing, gets no structural marker, and therefore gets no membership, which is the honest outcome and
requires no gatekeeping to arrange.

Everything downstream then stops being written per type. Membership, one-representability, the
MV-chain, and additive closure all become blanket impls conditioned on structural markers, so adding a
scale means adding a constructor and the facts that follow from its definition, and adding nothing
else. That is the same mechanism the law sketch already proved out, conditioning a blanket impl on a
type-level pair, applied one layer down.

This also settles the bias question rather than trading it off. Under a constructor-typed scheme a
bias parameter defaulting to `Zero` costs nothing when unused, and the additive-closure fact stops
being a caveat in prose: `AddClosed` is implemented for a zero bias and not otherwise, so folding a
biased format fails to resolve on its own merits.
