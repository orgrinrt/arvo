# Topic: what Warm and Precise do between 65 and 128 bits

**Date:** 2026-07-30
**Phase:** TOPIC
**Status:** described, not resolved. No changelist opens from this topic yet.

This records a conflict between the design and the source that was found while repairing an unrelated test.
It is written to be picked up cold by whoever next has reason to be in arvo, and deliberately stops at
description: the resolution is a design call with consumer-visible consequences, and nothing currently
depends on making it.

## What the source does

`tag_warm_precise` in `arvo-strategy/src/container.rs` maps a logical width to a container bucket. It
returns bucket 0 through 3 for widths up to 64, and bucket 5 for everything above, where bucket 5 is the
wide byte-sequence bucket. So `Warm` and `Precise` at 65 bits and beyond resolve to `WideBits`, exactly as
they do above 128.

Confirmed by compiling it rather than by reading it. `<Warm as BitsContainerFor<65, Unsigned>>::T` is
`WideBits<9>`.

## What the design says

Two places, and both say the opposite.

`arvo-strategy/DESIGN.md.tmpl` describes `Warm` as selecting containers matching Hot's footprint up to
native primitives, and then says "Above 128 bits, Warm uses `WideBits<{bytes_for(N)}>` (align-1
byte-exact)". Stating the wide bucket as what happens above 128 implies it is not what happens below.

The `#[diagnostic::on_unimplemented]` note on `BitsContainerFor` says it outright:

> Warm / Precise cover 1..=64 via 2x-logical primitives (u16/u32/u64/u128); for 65..=128 they have no
> native bucket and must use Hot or Cold. Above 128 bits all four strategies dispatch through
> `WideBits<bytes_for(N), A>`.

It closes by telling a consumer what to do "if this fires on Warm/Precise at N=65..=128". That branch of the
note is unreachable, because the bound it would fire on always resolves.

## Which is wrong

Design is the oracle, so the projection is what drifted. That is the starting point rather than the answer,
because the drift may be the more considered of the two: the wide bucket exists and works at those widths,
and refusing it would be removing a capability that currently ships.

Three shapes the resolution could take, and picking between them is the actual question.

**Refuse, as the diagnostic promises.** Restrict `tag_warm_precise` so 65 to 128 has no bucket, and a
consumer at those widths picks `Hot` or `Cold` explicitly. This makes the note true and the design's
implication true. It also stops `Warm` and `Precise` compiling at widths where they compile today, which is
a breaking change for any consumer already there.

**Allow, and correct both statements.** `Warm` and `Precise` reach every width, native to 64 and `WideBits`
above, the note loses its "must use Hot or Cold" advice, and the design says the wide bucket starts at 65
rather than 129. This is what ships today, so it costs nothing and admits that the 2x-logical contract
simply stops applying past the native ladder.

**Something between.** If the 2x-logical contract means something specific at 65 to 128 that neither
statement captures, the answer may be a wide bucket with different alignment, or a warning rather than a
refusal, per `arvo-toolbox-not-policer.md`: the substrate surfaces the consequence and the consumer decides.

## Why nothing caught it

The one test that named this boundary was `warm_bound_negative`, a compile-fail fixture asserting that
`UFixed<33, 32, Warm>` does not resolve. It had been failing on `E0425: cannot find function ibits` since it
was written, because it imported the `IBits` and `FBits` types while calling the `ibits` and `fbits`
functions, and its `.stderr` snapshot recorded that error as the expected one. It passed continuously while
asserting nothing about the container table.

Repairing the imports is what surfaced the conflict, and it also showed the test could not be repaired into
a useful one: its premise has no true instance at any width. It was removed in round `202607290130` rather
than left as a fixture pinning a name-resolution failure.

The lesson is worth keeping separately from the conflict itself. A compile-fail test proves only that the
code fails to compile, and says nothing about why unless the snapshot is read. A snapshot generated from
whatever error happened to occur will faithfully preserve the wrong one, and the test then guards the typo
instead of the contract. Reading the `.stderr` once, when it is first generated, is the whole defence.

## What resolving it costs

Whichever shape is chosen, three artifacts have to end up saying the same thing: the projection, the
diagnostic note, and the design document. Today no two of them agree.

It also needs a test either way, which is the part that does not exist in any form. If the answer is refuse,
that is a compile-fail case per strategy. If the answer is allow, it is a positive case asserting the
container resolves and the arithmetic behaves, at a width in the disputed band. The absence of a test in
either direction is why three artifacts could disagree for as long as they have.

## Scope note

Nothing depends on this today. Both strategies work at those widths, the arithmetic over `WideBits` is
exercised elsewhere, and no consumer in the workspace instantiates `Warm` or `Precise` in the disputed band.
It is recorded because a diagnostic that cannot fire and a document that contradicts the code are the
conditions under which the next person reasons confidently from the wrong one.
