# 190. Two false claims in a dispatch brief, and what they cost

The entailment check at `188` opened with something other than its verdict: two factual claims in the
brief that sent it were wrong. Both are mine. This records them where a later reader will meet them,
because a brief is not reviewed by anybody and the expert receiving one has no reason to doubt a
statement of fact from whoever assigned the work.

## The first, and it is the one that could have cost the dispatch its subject

The brief said, of the four axes `182` reported nothing declared for:

> **Two of them, and two more, have since been declared** (`ambient_domain`, `radix`,
> `accumulator_width`, `toolchain`). Check whether any of its unportable claims are now writable, and
> say which.

**None of them is.** `182` needed `declared_operand_window`, `representable_range_geometry`,
`encoding` and `constant_embedding_convention`. The axis pass landed a disjoint four, taken from
`183`'s ranking of what blocked the *later five topics*, which is a different corpus with different
blockers.

The mistake is a conflation of two seats' findings into one set, made while writing quickly, and it
is the exact shape the workspace names: two true statements welded into a connection neither
supports. `182` found four undeclared axes, true. Four axes were declared, true. They are not the
same four.

**What it would have cost had the check taken it.** The instruction attached to the false claim was
to go and find newly writable claims, so an expert obliging it would have spent its budget searching
for something that cannot exist, and might have concluded that the two law results still blocked by
`declared_operand_window` were writable and written them wrong. It read the axis file instead of the
brief, which is what a check is for.

## The second, smaller and the same class

The brief said the entailment checks were `54` and `75` on `63` and `74`. **`54` checks `53`**, the
container-derivation consolidation, which is a fifth consolidation and not among the four that were
ported. The check on `63` is `64`.

A file number carried from memory rather than read off an `ls`. The check read `64`, correctly,
because it went and looked.

## What follows for the next brief

**Any sentence of the form "X has since been done" is a claim about the tree and is one command
away.** So is a file number. The pre-dispatch discipline already says to check the cheap ones, and
the cheap ones are exactly these: a count, a "nobody has", a file name, a "since".

**The verification cost about ten seconds each and neither was done**, in a brief that spent four
paragraphs on how the expert should verify its own work. That asymmetry is the thing to notice: the
dispatcher's own claims went out unchecked inside an instruction to check everything.

## And one thing the check did that is worth as much as the findings

It drafted a severe finding, that one proposal's region had been lost, and **withdrew it after
verifying**. Every fact in the draft was right and the conclusion was wrong: the seven axes sit on
the law row the proposal wires to, in order, with their citation. It kept the withdrawal as a
committed probe rather than deleting it.

A report of nine findings where one is half wrong is the ordinary shape here, and it has already cost
this project a reverted legitimate decision once. Verifying before reporting is what stops that, and
it is the same act this note is about, pointed the other way.
