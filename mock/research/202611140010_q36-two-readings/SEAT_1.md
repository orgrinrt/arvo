## Reasoning by option

What the ratified rows settle before any option is looked at.

First, the codomain. "Arithmetic on a format is an exact operation in the ambient domain composed with a named total adaptation onto that set" (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1491`). A crossing is that shape with the exact operation the identity, so its adaptation lands on the target's set. All three options agree on this. The sentence fixes where the adaptation lands and is silent on who selects it. An earlier panel file claimed the sentence entailed the target's policy and withdrew the claim (`mock/research/202608072330_the-numeral-canon-panel/222_kiselyov_the_numeric_fundamentals.md:1567`); I reached the same limit before reading that file and agree with the withdrawal. So the spine alone picks nothing.

Second, what crosses. "A datum of a format is one point of the format's ambient domain" and a saturating end "is one point" (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1898`). The thing that enters a crossing is a point. It is not a residue class and it is not a point with a policy attached.

Third, admission. "An operation is admitted exactly when it is a function of the declared signature, and where two realisations of one name disagree, the signature is missing a coordinate" (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1583`). Options 1 and 2 are each a function of the two endpoint signatures, so the admission rule admits both and selects neither. What it does forbid is leaving the matter unstated: two realisations of one crossing name that disagree on 226 of 256 values cannot both ship under that name.

Fourth, transport. "A predicate on its base, which a construction never transports for free and which must be derived per construction" (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1856`). I use this against option 1 below, and I flag that the row speaks of constructions over algebras, so applying it to an adaptation carried from one set to another is my extension.

Fifth, the declared mode. On release "what stands in its place is the guarantee plus an explicit declaration of the mode, saturate or wrap or whatever applies to the overflow or underflow at hand" (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1095`). The mode is declared explicitly. The row does not say where, and it asks to be read loosely.

Nothing else ratified bears with force. Two rows at rung `stated`, which bind as direction and are not canon, are cited where they lean.

The measured cell. By hand: signed eight bits into signed four bits. 240 of 256 source values lie outside the target's range. Wrapping and saturating agree off-range exactly when the wrapped value lands on the end the clamp would pick, which is seven values congruent to the top above the range and seven congruent to the bottom below it, so 240 less 14 is 226. The model reproduces it (`q36_model.out:3`), and gives 225 for unsigned eight into unsigned four and 128 of 128 for a change of signedness at eight bits (`q36_model.out:4` to `:6`). Over all 256 width and signedness cells from one to eight bits, no value inside the target's range is treated differently by the two completions (`q36_model.out`, section E7), which is the question's own scoping confirmed.

Option 1, the source's.

Ratified text for it: none directly. The admission rule admits it as a function of declared signatures (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1583`). The ratified statement that wrapping carries a group law and saturation does not (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1833`, row `a_min_plus_top_absorbs_addition_under_saturation_and_never_under_wrapping`) is the nearest thing to support: a value produced by wrapped arithmetic is trustworthy only modulo the source's span, and reduction modulo a divisor of that span is the one narrowing that respects this.

Ratified text against it, four arguments.

A1, the source's adaptation is the wrong object. By the spine an adaptation is a map "onto that set" (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1491`). The source's adaptation is a map onto the source's set, and by H1 it is the identity on every value that could cross. It cannot govern a loss into another set. What option 1 actually applies is a third thing: the source's policy name carried onto the target's set. That is a pair of set and adaptation that nobody declared, neither the source's signature nor the target's, while behaviour is "stated per declared signature" (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1514`). And the carried pair need not be admitted: by the two-expert proposal at `mock/registry/proposal.toml:2444`, wrapping is admitted only where the set is one arithmetic progression, so a wrapping source crossing into a float-like target has no adaptation to use under option 1 at all. Option 1 is therefore partial where option 2 is total, since a target's signature is an admitted pair by construction. This half of A1 rests on a proposal and on my extension of `mock/registry/ruling.toml:1856`; mark it as such.

A2, arity. The canonical lossy crossing in the canon is the one inside the spine itself: an exact result in the ambient domain adapted onto a set. Its source has no selected reduction. An operation with several operands of differing signatures has several. The target is always exactly one. The unratified row at `mock/registry/proposal.toml:411` says conversion and resolution are one obligation at two arities; if that is right, "the source's" is not a well-defined selector at arity two and "the target's" is. I use the proposal only to name the observation; the observation itself needs nothing but the spine.

A3, the factoring. Define the target's own operation as the spine does: adapt the exact result onto the target's set by the target's adaptation. Hold the exact result losslessly in a wider format and cross it into the target. Under option 2 the crossing returns the target's own answer for every operand pair. That is true by construction, and I do not present the zeros in the model as evidence. The content is the other column: under option 1 the same crossing disagrees with the target's own operation whenever the wide format's policy differs from the target's. In the model, integers at four bits through a ten-bit exact intermediate: 64 of 256 sums and 145 of 256 products differ when the completions differ (`q36_model.out`, section E3). Fixed point at six bits and two fraction bits through an exact fourteen-bit intermediate: 1024 of 4096 sums, 2493 to 2881 of 4096 products, and 380 of 4096 products from the rounding mode alone when both sides saturate (`q36_model.out:56` to `:62`). So under option 1 a consumer who widens, computes exactly and narrows does not get the narrow type's arithmetic, and the spine's factoring is not realisable by a crossing.

A4, a lossless step is not inert under option 1. Insert between source and target a format whose set contains the source's. Under option 2 the result is unchanged for every policy triple, by proof: the first step is the identity on points by H1 and the second is the same map as the direct crossing. The model shows zero over 224 cells (`q36_model.out`, section E8) and that zero is constructional. Under option 1 the inserted step changes the answer on 226 of 256 values whenever the intermediate's policy differs from the source's (`q36_model.out`, section E2), and on 8576 values in total over the same 224 cells. Under option 1 a point is not what crosses: the point together with the policy of the last type it sat in is. That sits badly with a datum being one point (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1898`). I do not overstate this: a change of signature changes later arithmetic under every option, so the fact that the type matters is not itself a defect. The defect is narrower. Under option 1 the loss at a crossing is decided by where the value has been, and under option 2 by where it is going.

What option 1 does to a consumer. At a lossy crossing the consumer reads the target's type, sees its declared policy, and gets something else. The row's own cost says this reads badly at an ingest boundary (`mock/registry/question.toml:786`); the two ingest rows are at rung `stated` and one was explicitly not blessed (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1023`, `mock/registry/ruling.toml:1996`), so I draw nothing from them. To get the target's policy the consumer first moves the value to a sibling of the source under the target's policy, which is the undeclared pair of A1 run in the other direction.

Option 2, the target's.

Ratified text for it: the codomain (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1491`), which is necessary and not sufficient; behaviour stated per declared signature (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1514`), since the adaptation used is exactly the one the target's signature declares over the target's own set, so no undeclared pair is formed; the datum as a point (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1898`), by A4; and the explicit declaration of the mode (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1095`), since the target signature written at the crossing is that declaration. At rung `stated`: the overflow behaviour "shouldn't be called one way", with a shipped default "easy to override per case" (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1932` and the quote below it). Option 2 with a sibling target is that shape. Direction, not canon.

Ratified text against it: none that I found. Two things weigh against it and neither is ratified text.

The first is arithmetic, and my own model produced it against my expectation. I predicted that option 2 would make the crossing a homomorphism for addition more often than option 1. It does not. From a wrapping eight-bit source into a saturating four-bit target, the law h(a + b) = h(a) + h(b), each sum taken in its own system, holds on 49152 of 65536 pairs when the crossing wraps (option 1) and on 16992 when it saturates (option 2). In the opposite cell option 2 wins by 50056 to 912 (`q36_model.out`, section E4). Read together the four cells say something neither endpoint rule says: the wrapping crossing preserves addition better than the saturating one in every cell, including the two where it is neither endpoint's selection (37740 against 33376 when both endpoints saturate). So preservation of the source's arithmetic is not a criterion that selects an endpoint. It selects a completion, and it is the honest content of the row's stated cost for option 2, "the route on which a wide wrapping value saturates" (`mock/registry/question.toml:787`). I report it as a refuted prediction and as the strongest thing on option 1's side.

The second is the standards bound. A panel file argues that MATLAB attaches rounding and overflow to the `fimath` object and not to either operand's numeric type, and reads that as evidence for option 3 against option 2 (`mock/research/202608072330_the-numeral-canon-panel/221_dolan_the_numeric_fundamentals.md:818`, unratified). The parity bound is ratified (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1567`) and the adequacy obligation is in the registry (`mock/registry/obligation.toml:196`). My answer: the objection assumes the target is a numeric type without a policy. Where the target is a pair of set and adaptation, a `fimath`-governed crossing is a crossing onto the signature whose adaptation is that `fimath`, which is option 2 at a named target. As a claim about code, the format crate's conversion takes exactly such a pair (`mock/crates/arvo-format/src/conversion.rs:233`), so the convention is expressible there for every mode the crate has. I did not build the alias, and as a claim about code no parity test in the tree exercises a crossing (`mock/crates/arvo-format/tests/matlab_fimath_parity.rs` contains no conversion call that I could find by search). My recollection of how MATLAB, IEEE 754, SystemC, C and Rust assign the policy at a conversion is that none of them takes the source's and all take the destination's or the site's; that is recollection, I had no network and verified none of it here, and nothing in this file rests on it.

What option 2 does to a consumer. A lossy crossing gives what the written target type says. A consumer who wants another reduction names a sibling of the target carrying it and then moves to the target itself, a step that is lossless and so needs no order. Every pair of target set and admitted adaptation is reachable this way, including pairs that are neither endpoint's selection, which is the case the row's distinguisher asks about (`mock/registry/question.toml:792`). The cost is real: a consumer narrowing a genuinely modular quantity, a hash or a sequence number, into a saturating type gets a clamp unless they say otherwise, and nothing stops them. A crossing through a lossy intermediate is not the direct crossing under either endpoint rule (`q36_model.out:100` to `:104`), and under one policy throughout it still differs for the two half modes, on 30 to 32 of 256 values in the modelled chain (`q36_model.out:90` to `:93`), while the three directed modes compose on nested grids (`q36_model.out`, section E5). So "one placement, never a chain" is a separate fact about rounding that option 2 does not supply and does not need.

Option 3, a third named at the crossing site.

Ratified text for it: the adaptation is "named" and "first-class" (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1491`); the mode is an "explicit declaration" (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1095`); and the admission rule's remark that disagreeing realisations mean "the signature is missing a coordinate" (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1583`), which can be read as saying the crossing's own signature must carry the adaptation. The homomorphism counts above are arithmetic support: there are cells where the better crossing is neither endpoint's.

Ratified text against it as an exclusive rule: none. At rung `stated`, the direction that a default ships and is overridden per case (`mock/registry/ruling.toml:1932`) weighs against the row's own stated cost, "no default for a consumer who writes nothing" (`mock/registry/question.toml:788`).

The finding about option 3 is that it is not a third position once the target is a pair of set and adaptation. Naming a reduction at the crossing site is naming a sibling of the target, and the crossing is then governed by its target's adaptation. Option 3 equals option 2 applied at a named sibling followed by a lossless step. What remains of option 3 as a distinct rule is only the prohibition: no crossing may lean on the eventual target's own adaptation, every lossy site must spell one. That buys nothing a consumer cannot already write and costs the ceremony the row names. And a crossing always names its target, so under option 2 the choice is already visible where it is made.

Is the reduction one thing. The question says "reduction" in the singular. An adaptation has a rounding part and a completion part, and where both lose, the endpoints offer four functions and not two: in the model, an eight-bit source at two fraction bits into a four-bit integer target with differing modes and differing completions gives four distinct endpoint-derived functions, and ten distinct functions over the ten modelled policies (`q36_model.out`, section E6). The row's three options do not mention the mixed readings, rounding from one side and completion from the other. I did not examine them further. Arguments A1 to A4 apply to each part separately and give the target's for both, so I read the pick as covering both parts, and I flag that the row never asked.

Why a pick and not `none`. The ratified rows do not force option 2; they exclude nothing but silence. I pick because the task permits the arithmetic to speak where the rows stop, and because the three positions are not symmetric once examined: option 1 needs an object the spine does not supply (A1), has no meaning at the spine's own crossing (A2), breaks the factoring when realised through a wider format (A3), and makes a lossless step change a later loss (A4), while option 3 reduces to option 2 at a sibling. The answer flips in two places and the panel should know them. If preservation of a wrapping source's arithmetic is taken as the criterion, the answer is a completion, wrapping wherever admitted, and no endpoint. If the premise falls and no endpoint owns an adaptation, the answer is the site, which in arvo's terms is again the signature the crossing lands on.

## What a target-only conversion commits to

These are claims about code and design, all unratified.

The value crate's method is `convert<T: Sole>(self) -> Num<T, C>` on `Num<S, C>` (`mock/crates/arvo-numeral/src/ratio.rs:83`). Its shape alone commits to less than it appears to, and its body commits to more.

The shape alone. A method that takes a target signature and nothing else can still read two signatures, its own and the target's, each with an adaptation. So the shape does not decide between option 1 and option 2: the same Rust signature would compile with a body that applied the source's policy on the target's grid. What the shape does commit to is three things. One, there is a default: a lossy crossing exists that a consumer can write with no reduction named beyond the endpoints, which rules out option 3 read as a mandatory third. Two, the reduction is a function of the two endpoint signatures and of nothing at the site. Three, lossy and lossless crossings share one name, so the type system does not mark the sites where an order is owed; whether a given value loses is asked at run time by `fits_convert` (`mock/crates/arvo-numeral/src/ratio.rs:106`), and that bears on the open row at `mock/registry/question.toml:812` more than on Q36.

The body and what stands behind it. The method calls the format crate's conversion with the source's format and the target's signature (`mock/crates/arvo-numeral/src/ratio.rs:100`), and its documentation says a member "is rounded by `T`'s mode" and "completed by `T`'s overflow policy" (`mock/crates/arvo-numeral/src/ratio.rs:72` to `:73`). The format crate's function is `convert<F: Format, S: DeclaredSignature>` (`mock/crates/arvo-format/src/conversion.rs:233`): the source is a bare format with no adaptation, and the operation's declared signature is the target's (`mock/crates/arvo-format/src/conversion.rs:54` to `:58`). At that layer option 1 is not merely unchosen, it is unwritable, because the function has no way to learn the source's policy. The design states the same as settled fact (`mock/crates/arvo-numeral/DESIGN.md.tmpl:332` to `:334`), and the value crate's test says in a comment that "the source's adaptation takes no part in a conversion" (`mock/crates/arvo-numeral/tests/the_conversion.rs:85`) with an oracle that lands under the target (`mock/crates/arvo-numeral/tests/common/oracle.rs:139` to `:141`). So the stack as shipped is committed to option 2 at four places: a function signature, a method body, a design paragraph and a test oracle.

What it does not foreclose. Because the target is a full signature and a lossless move between sibling signatures exists (`under`, described at `mock/crates/arvo-numeral/DESIGN.md.tmpl:416` to `:440`, with the sibling aliases at `mock/crates/arvo-format/src/adapt.rs:84` and `:92`), a consumer can name any admitted reduction at the site. The shipped shape therefore forecloses option 1 as the meaning of the plain crossing and forecloses option 3 as an obligation, and it forecloses nothing a consumer could want to express, apart from the stochastic mode, whose dither the value method does not pass (`mock/crates/arvo-numeral/src/ratio.rs:100` passes `Dither::UNUSED`).

The standing of that commitment. Q36 is open, with the panel as decider and no `answered` field (`mock/registry/question.toml:782` to `:794`). The only row behind the shipped behaviour is a proposal at `one_expert` that declares no `answers` edge to Q36 (`mock/registry/proposal-the-exact-operations.toml:29` to `:36`), and the numeral design does not cite the question. A design answered an open canon question by shipping. That my reading lands on the same option does not repair it: the code is not evidence for option 2, the agreement between the proposal, the design, the code and the test is four copies of one author's stance and not corroboration, and my pick is one more unratified reading until a second seat and the gate say otherwise. If the panel answers otherwise the conversion comes off the branch; if it answers option 2 the design owes the citation it never carried.
