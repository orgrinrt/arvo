## The posture for the coming four: attempt what looks unsolvable

Op:

> Good progress, but let's be novel here too, or at least attempt to find solutions that seem
> unsolvable, but only in lack of prior art on it. We might find ergonomics or efficiency wins here
> via this outlook, and in worst case, just fall back to what we've been doing and find solutions
> anyway.

This is the load-bearing instruction of this checkpoint and it outranks the three calls above where
they conflict.

The distinction it draws is between two kinds of "cannot":

- **Cannot, because the thing is impossible.** A constraint of the type system, of monomorphisation,
  of the forbidden-feature list, or of mathematics. These are real and are not what this is about.
- **Cannot, because nobody has done it.** No paper names it, no library ships it, no vocabulary
  exists for it. This is not a constraint. It is an absence, and it is where the wins are.

A member that meets the second kind and reports it as a limitation has stopped one step early.
Attempt it. Build the thing that has no prior art, compile it, and see what happens. The specific
hope is **ergonomics and efficiency**: a shape that is nicer to write or cheaper to lower than the
one the literature would have handed over.

The downside is bounded and stated: if the attempt fails, fall back to the approach the review has
been using and solve it that way regardless. A failed novel attempt costs one section and leaves a
compiled negative result, which this review has repeatedly found more useful than an untested
argument. So the expected value is one-sided and the attempt is not a gamble.

What this does **not** license is permuting the axes already in hand and calling it novelty, or
proposing a mechanism without compiling it. The bar for a novel proposal is the same as for any
other: it exists as code, it was run, and its cost is stated.
